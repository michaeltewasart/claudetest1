Breakfast in Oaxaca: seven moles and a market at dawn

We followed a family of cooks through the Mercado 20 de Noviembre to learn why a single mole can take three days and more than thirty ingredients.

By Lena Ortiz12 min read

A market before sunrise

The stalls are still half-lit when the first comal heats up. Vendors stack dried chiles in towering baskets: smoky pasilla, fruity ancho, the almost-black chilhuacle that gives mole negro its depth. The air smells of toasted sesame and wood smoke.

Doña Rosa has cooked here for more than thirty years. She greets regulars by name and hands out spoonfuls of mole on squares of tortilla, waiting to see which one makes people close their eyes.

Why seven?

Oaxaca is often called the land of seven moles: negro, rojo, coloradito, amarillo, verde, chichilo and manchamantel. Each has its own balance of chiles, spices, nuts, seeds and fruit, and each family guards its own version.

The common thread is patience. Chiles are toasted one by one, seeds are roasted until they just begin to pop, and everything is ground into a paste that is then fried and simmered for hours until it turns glossy and thick.

The three-day mole

For celebrations, Doña Rosa starts on Thursday. Day one is for toasting and grinding. Day two is for frying the paste and slowly loosening it with stock. Day three is for tasting, adjusting and letting the flavors settle.

"Mole is not a sauce," she tells us, stirring a pot wide enough to bathe in. "It is a reason for everyone to come home."

Try it at home

  • Start with a good store-bought mole paste and loosen it with warm chicken stock.
  • Add a square of dark chocolate and a pinch of cinnamon to round it out.
  • Simmer at least 20 minutes, stirring often, until it coats a spoon.
  • Serve over poached chicken with rice and warm tortillas.
